Australian heritage

Listed, But Is It Safe?

Australia protects hundreds of thousands of heritage places. This page explores what is on the list, where it is, and whether being listed is enough to keep it safe.

Uluru, Uluru-Kata Tjuta National Park. Photo: Journey Beyond Rail

01

What is on the list?

The National Heritage List started in 2004. It is reserved for places with “outstanding heritage value to the nation”, and every place is listed as one of three types: natural (landscapes, reefs, fossils and wildlife), historic (buildings, sites and events that shaped the nation) or Indigenous (places important to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples).

232,000 Indigenous sites recorded
15,000 Historic places on state registers
8,800 Underwater sites
389 Commonwealth places
126 National Heritage places
21 World Heritage properties

Heritage in Australia works like a pyramid. At the bottom are hundreds of thousands of sites recorded by the states. Near the top are the 126 National Heritage places, which the Australian Government is responsible for, and above them the 21 World Heritage properties. This page focuses on the National Heritage List: these are meant to be the best protected places in the country, so if they are not safe, nothing below them is either.

The National Heritage family tree

The List branches into its three heritage types, then into states and territories, then into all 126 places. More than half are historic places, and only 20 are listed for their Indigenous values, even though Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures go back more than 65,000 years. Where is it?

National Heritage places are not spread evenly. Most sit in the south-east, while some jurisdictions have only a handful of listings. Hover for counts, then click a state to compare it with the rest.

Every National Heritage place in Australia

Source: DCCEEW, National Heritage List Spatial Database

Each dot is a National Heritage place. This map shows 119 of the 126. The other seven are in external territories or on Macquarie Island.

Zoom in on the south-east and the map fills up. 73 places, six in every ten, sit in this corner of the country, and 56 of them are historic: convict sites, colonial buildings and places tied to early settlement around Sydney, Melbourne and Hobart.

Now look north of the Tropic of Capricorn. Across the whole tropical north there are only 14 places. Nine are natural, like Kakadu and the Great Barrier Reef, and five are Indigenous. Not one is historic.

Only 20 places are listed for their Indigenous values, spread thinly across every state, even though Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have cared for this whole continent for more than 65,000 years.

03

How has protection expanded?

Protection grows in three ways: new places join the National Heritage List, states add places to their own registers, and new sites are recorded on land and underwater. But is it growing fast enough, and evenly?

National Heritage listings over time

75 of the 126 places (60%) were listed in the first four years, including 16 World Heritage places added on a single day in 2007. Since 2008, only about three places have been added each year. Drag across the lower chart to zoom in.

Source: DCCEEW, National Heritage List Spatial Database

Historic places added to state registers each year

States added 402 historic places to their registers in five years, but the effort is uneven: Queensland added 90, while Tasmania added only 6. Circle area shows the number of places. Use the dropdown to see each financial year.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, heritage data

On land or underwater? Heritage added to registers, 2016–17 to 2019–20

Over the same four years, 428 underwater sites were added, compared with 291 historic places on land. New South Wales alone added 224 underwater sites, more than half the national total, while Western Australia added none despite its long coastline. The ACT has no coastline, and the Australian Government manages underwater sites in Commonwealth waters rather than a historic places register. No underwater data was reported for 2015–16.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, heritage data

What gets recorded? Indigenous heritage sites added to Queensland’s database, 2012–2020

Queensland recorded 15,164 new Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander heritage sites in nine years, and three in four are artefact scatters (stone tools and flakes left on the ground). 04

Are these places actually safe?

A listing is a promise, not a guarantee. It only helps if there are laws that work, a plan to manage the place, and people with the power to look after it. Here is what the people closest to these places say.

Do Indigenous stakeholders think heritage is protected by law?

Each square is one in every 100 respondents. Almost all of them said Australian laws do not adequately protect Indigenous heritage. This was a survey of Indigenous stakeholders for the 2021 State of the Environment report, not of all Indigenous Australians.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, Indigenous stakeholder survey

What Indigenous stakeholders see as the biggest pressures

Protecting cultural sites (72%) and protecting Indigenous knowledge (69%) top the list, well ahead of climate change (43%). Respondents could choose more than one option, so the totals add up to more than 100%.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, Indigenous stakeholder survey

Listed, but is anyone managing it? Management plans for the 120 National Heritage places, 2023

Only 73 of the 120 places (61%) have a management plan that their own managers rate as effective. For 35 places, there is either no plan or no answer at all. Each bar shows how many places are left at that step, and the red notes show how many drop out. Effectiveness is self-reported by place managers. The department later confirmed that 6 of the 17 places without a plan have one in place or in preparation. “Effectiveness not rated” is calculated from the report text (85 plans minus 77 ratings).

Source: DCCEEW (2023), Review of the National Heritage List and Commonwealth Heritage List, Appendix F

Case study: Juukan Gorge

In May 2020, mining company Rio Tinto blasted two rock shelters at Juukan Gorge in the Pilbara, Western Australia. The shelters held evidence of around 46,000 years of human occupation and were deeply important to the Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura peoples.

The blast was legal. Rio Tinto had been given consent in 2013 under Western Australia’s Aboriginal Heritage Act 1972, and the site was not on the National Heritage List. An excavation in 2014 found the shelters were older and more significant than first thought, but the law had no way to reverse a consent once it was given. Federal law and native title rights did not stop it either.

“We are burning our libraries.”

Dr Virginia Marshall, Wiradjuri Nyemba legal academic

A federal parliamentary inquiry followed. Its interim report was titled Never Again, and its final report, A Way Forward (October 2021), made 8 recommendations. Western Australia passed a new heritage law in 2021, then repealed it in 2023 and went back to the 1972 Act.

What is threatening them?

For the 2021 State of the Environment report, heritage experts were asked which pressures have the greatest impact on each type of heritage. Their answers fall into five groups: climate change, industry, population, governance and other.

What do experts say is hurting heritage most?

For natural heritage, climate change is the biggest worry (41%). But for geoheritage, Indigenous and historic heritage, the top group is governance: too little money and weak legal protection. The single most named pressure overall is inadequate resourcing. Column width shows how many mentions each heritage type received. Click any tile to see the pressures behind it.

Source: McConnell (2021a), heritage expert survey, Australia State of the Environment 2021

Case study: Ningaloo Coast, Western Australia

Ningaloo, on the remote coast of Western Australia, has one of the longest near-shore reefs in the world and some of the largest gatherings of whale sharks. It was World Heritage listed in 2011, and IUCN says it is generally well protected. Even so, climate change is now rated its greatest threat, and in 2025 the reef went through its worst heat stress ever recorded.

*Projections assume global emissions keep rising (a “business-as-usual” scenario).

Source: IUCN World Heritage Outlook (2025); Heron et al. (2017, 2018)

06

Can conservation keep up?

Looking after heritage costs money: for repairs, for management plans and for research. The Australian Heritage Grants program funds projects at National Heritage places, and Round 8 (2025–26) shows where that money went.

Where the money goes: Australian Heritage Grants, Round 8 (2025–26)

Round 8 funded 20 projects worth $5.2 million. Historic places received 55% and natural places 42%, but only one project ($0.12 million, about 2%) went to a place listed for its Indigenous values. Some projects at natural places, such as Willandra Lakes, are Indigenous-led, so this shows how places are listed rather than every dollar that helps Indigenous heritage. Projects are grouped by the main purpose in their description. Amounts exclude GST. Hover over a flow for details.

Source: DCCEEW, Australian Heritage Grants Round 8 successful projects

The big picture: who gets the money to manage World Heritage?

Between 2011–12 and 2015–16, the Australian Government spent $485 million managing World Heritage properties. Just three of them, the Great Barrier Reef, Kakadu and Uluru-Kata Tjuta, received 81%. Ningaloo Coast received $3.2 million (0.7%). Bars show each property’s funding, largest first, and the line shows the running total. Hover for details, or pick a single financial year. Heard and McDonald Islands received no funding in this period, so it is not shown. Amounts in millions of dollars; this is older data than the rest of the page.

Source: Department of the Environment and Energy, World Heritage management funding 2011–12 to 2015–16

Australia has listed 126 places of outstanding value to the nation, and hundreds of thousands more sit on state registers. But the data tells us that a listing is only the start. Most national listings happened in the first few years. Almost every Indigenous stakeholder surveyed says the law does not protect their heritage. Almost a third of National Heritage places cannot show an effective management plan, and experts say the biggest gap is resourcing. Juukan Gorge and Ningaloo show what is at stake when protection falls short.

Listed is not the same as safe.

About the data

National Heritage places come from the public National Heritage List spatial database, which lists 126 places. Each place is listed as one heritage type (natural, historic or Indigenous), even when it holds more than one kind of value.

Survey results come from the 2021 State of the Environment report. The Indigenous stakeholder survey reflects the people who responded, not all Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians, and respondents could choose more than one answer. The expert survey counts how often each pressure was named, not how severe it is.

Management plan effectiveness is self-reported by place managers. “Effectiveness not rated” is calculated from the report text (85 plans minus 77 ratings).

The Queensland heatmap covers Queensland only, and its colour scale is compressed (symlog) so smaller counts stay visible. World Heritage funding covers 2011–12 to 2015–16, older than the rest of the page. Ningaloo projections assume global emissions keep rising. Grant amounts exclude GST.
